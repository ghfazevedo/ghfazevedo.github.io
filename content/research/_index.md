title: Research
lead: "I study how evolutionary processes connect across scales, from population genomics to species diversification. Integrating phylogenomics, coalescent theory and macroevolutionary modeling, I ask how molecular-level processes shape trait evolution, speciation and biodiversity patterns. My research follows three lines:  

1. [how genomes, environment and performance-related traits interact to drive diversification across micro- and macroevolutionary scales;](#program-1)  

2. [how population-level processes, informed by genomic data, reveal phenotypic evolution across deep time;](#program-2)  

3. [how gene flow between divergent species shapes the evolution of sex-related traits and diversification.](#program-3)  
  
  
To address these questions, I combine natural history collections, fieldwork and molecular lab work with bioinformatics and statistical modeling.  
  
  
Underlying all three lines is a continued commitment to systematics and taxonomy, the disciplines that generate the hypotheses of relationships and species boundaries on which ecological and evolutionary research depends."

programs:
  - title: Unraveling the interplay between environment, genes, traits and diversification across scales 
    image: program-1.png
    summary: Linking genetic, phenotypic and ecological variation to how lineages form, persist and become species.
    text: >-
      The interactions among genotypes, phenotypes and environment determine the fate of lineages over evolutionary time. I develop and apply analytical tools that link micro- and macroevolution to understand how this interplay drives diversification, maintains diversity or leads to extinction.  
      For example, my Trait-Dependent Rates Protracted Speciation model tests how traits and environment jointly shape population divergence, extinction risk and speciation completion. In cave spiders, it showed that cave specialization affects extinction risk in taxon-specific ways. In Brazilian wandering spiders (Phoneutria), I combined molecular, phenotypic and environmental data to ask which selective forces keep lineages distinct despite incomplete reproductive isolation. At deeper timescales, I have paired fossils, including micro-CT scans, with phylogenomics to connect geological change to faunal turnover and present-day biodiversity.  
  - title: Phenotypic evolution through the lens of population genetics processes
    image: program-2.png
    summary: Homoplasy, hemiplasy and the models that tell them apart when genealogies and species trees disagree.
    text: >-
      Beyond resolving phylogenies, phylogenomic data can reveal how molecular evolution and population genetics shape phenotypic evolution, a link rarely considered in trait-evolution studies.
      In two-clawed spiders, I showed that traits associated with a web-less lifestyle can look like convergence when they may instead reflect hemiplasy. Hemiplasy is a mismatch between trait genealogies and the species tree, caused by population-level processes such as drift or ancestral balancing selection. Building on this, I developed PhUNC, a simulation-based framework that combines coalescent and trait-evolution models to estimate the probability of hemiplasy. I applied it to test whether drift alone can explain the fixation of alternative color phenotypes across Phoneutria lineages, or whether selection is required.
  - title: Introgression, sexual selection, speciation & phenotypic diversity
    image: program-3.png
    summary: Introgression and the evolution of courtship signals and other traits across the spider ~~tree~~ web of life.
    text: >-
      Exaggerated sexual traits are usually seen as markers of genetic isolation and species identity. Yet gene flow between such species appears more common than previously thought, a contradiction that calls for a closer look at the processes behind it. In rapidly radiating clades, introgression can add to incomplete lineage sorting and further obscure phenotypic signal.
      My phylogenomic work on harmochirine jumping spiders revealed these conditions in Habronattus, which diversified into more than 100 species in under 5 million years. In Australian peacock spiders (Maratus), courtship ornaments may have evolved independently several times, with some patterns possibly blurred by introgression.
      With collaborators in the USA and Canada, I am analyzing genomic and transcriptomic data from about 120 Habronattus species and forms. We are finding strong signs of introgression between species with strikingly different multimodal sexual signals, which may also be generating new morphotypes and ecotypes. To detect introgression deep in the phylogeny, I am developing ARAQNA (Ancestral Reticulation Assessment via Quartet-frequency Nodal Anomalies), which combines coalescent simulation with machine-learning classification.

In essence, my research bridges micro- and macroevolution to resolve patterns that otherwise seem intricate or contradictory, while developing the interdisciplinary tools needed to understand the biodiversity around us. Collections-grounded knowledge of what shaped past biodiversity can help guide its conservation for future generations.
